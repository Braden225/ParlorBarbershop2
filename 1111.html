03The fade

Everything a barber gets judged on happens on one line.

A fade is not a length. It is a transition — the place where the short stops being short, and how gradually it gets there. Set that line too high and the cut wears you. Too low and it disappears in a week. Blend it badly and everyone can see the step from across a room, including you, in a mirror, for the next two weeks.

So we ask before we start. Where do you want the line to sit, how hard do you want the contrast, and how much of it do you actually want to still be there two weeks from now. Then we cut to that, not to whatever is on the screen you brought in.

04In the chair

What actually happens, start to finish.

  1. 1

    You book, or you call.

    Booking holds a named slot with a named barber. Calling gets you a person who can tell you what is open this week, which is usually faster than scrolling. Either way, say what you want done — a cut and a beard needs more time on the book than a cut alone.

  2. 2

    We talk before anything is switched on.

    Two minutes, standing up, in front of the mirror. What you liked about the last one and what you didn’t. Where your hair grows the wrong way, because it does and pretending otherwise is how people end up disappointed. If what you are asking for will not sit right on your head, you will hear that now rather than at the end.

  3. 3

    The cut.

    Clippers where clippers belong, shears where they don’t. The line gets set first and checked against your head shape, not against a guard number. Beard work, if you asked for it, comes after the cut so the two agree with each other.

  4. 4

    You look, and you say if it’s wrong.

    Chair turned, mirror behind. This is the moment to speak up, and we would rather you did — taking a little more off takes thirty seconds. Then we tell you roughly when to come back: a tight fade stops looking like a fade in about two weeks, a longer cut will give you five or six.

05The shop

Fort Worth Avenue, cutting for Oak Cliff.

We are on Fort Worth Avenue, between Oak Cliff proper and the bridges into downtown. Most of the people in these chairs live within about ten minutes of them. Some have been coming long enough that they no longer say what they want — they sit down and their barber already knows.

It is a barbershop, so it sounds like one. Clippers, the door, three conversations going at once and somebody’s kid in the second chair with a cape on, waiting to be told to sit still. Nobody is trying to make it feel like a spa.

07Straight answers

The questions people actually ask.

Do you take walk-ins?

Booking is the reliable way in. Walk-in availability changes hour to hour depending on who is in and how the book looks, so the honest answer is: call the shop first. We would rather tell you it is a bad afternoon than have you drive over and wait.

Do you cut children’s hair?

Yes, and regularly — parents bring children in alongside their own appointments. If it is a first haircut, say so when you book. It usually goes better with a bit of extra time on the clock and nobody standing over the chair telling anyone to hurry up.

So what is it going to cost me?

A haircut is posted at $40 and a beard added to it at $10, on barber Eddie Sanchez’s rates. Rates can vary between barbers, so ask when you book and you will get a number before you sit down rather than a surprise afterwards. We do not add anything you did not agree to.

Are hot towel shaves still on?

Ask for it at the time of booking rather than on arrival. A proper shave needs the chair held for longer than a cut, and it needs a barber who is set up for it that day. Booked ahead, it is a straightforward yes.

I don’t really know what to ask for.

Then say that, out loud, at the start. It is a better opening than a photograph of somebody with a different head. Tell us how you wore it when you liked it, how much time you are willing to spend on it in the morning, and how long you want to go between visits. That is enough for a barber to work from.
